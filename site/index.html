    From breadboard to finished robot

    I did every part myself: the body, the electronics, and the software. These are the stages, in order.

    1. Proteus Checked the obstacle-avoidance logic in a circuit simulator first.

    2. Breadboard Wired the controller, motor driver, sensor, and motors together to test them for real.

    3. SolidWorks Designed the 35 cm round body, the debris chamber, and the mounts, then 3D-printed them in PLA+.

    4. KiCad Designed a two-layer, 100 × 50 mm circuit board to replace the loose wiring.

    5. Assembly Fitted the motors, fan, battery, and electronics inside the shell.

    6. Underside Two drive wheels, a caster, and the intake that feeds the debris chamber.

    The hardest bug: a smarter controller that stalled the motors

    Two cheap motors never spin at exactly the same speed, so a robot told to go straight drifts. I added wheel sensors and a feedback controller (a PID controller) that pushes harder on whichever wheel falls behind.

    It worked until the robot turned. The controller asked for so much current that the supply voltage sagged and a motor stalled, and the robot stopped dead.

    The fix was to let a simple model of each motor supply most of the power (feed-forward) and keep the feedback controller for small corrections only. The robot stayed stable, in exchange for slightly less precise steering.

    What it can't do yet

    • It doesn't map the room. It turns left at every obstacle, so it can miss spots, as the room at the top shows.
    • It has no brushes and no flap to hold debris in, so it picks up only light, loose dirt.
    • Any phone in Bluetooth range can connect and drive it; there is no pairing.
    • A disconnected distance sensor reads as a clear path.
    • Turns have no time limit, so a failed wheel sensor during a turn keeps it spinning.

    For engineers

    The firmware is one MicroPython file on a Raspberry Pi Pico W, running a 20 ms control loop. After the thesis I added 42 host-side tests that run the real firmware against fake hardware, continuous integration, and fixes for five control-logic bugs, each found with a failing test first.
